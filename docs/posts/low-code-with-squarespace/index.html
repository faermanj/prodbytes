Delivering Low-Code Projects with Squarespace

2026-08-04

In this video, I want to talk about no-code and low-code solutions you can use to deliver your projects. E super familiar with code, and that’s a bit of why I’m here, but that doesn’t mean code is the solution for everything. Sometimes a low-code or no-code tool is the easiest way to deliver just what you need.

This one is especially for you if you’re coming into technology from another area, or if you’re just not familiar with coding solutions yet. Maybe you’re thinking that you need code for everything, and I want to show you that it’s not always the case. Of course, learning to code helps, and we go deeper on that throughout this group. But you can have a real, working digital presence today, without writing a line of it.

The tool for today is Squarespace. I’m not sponsored or affiliated with them in any way. It’s just what I use personally, so it’s what I can honestly share. We’ll look at some alternatives too.

A real project, not a demo

The website I want to show is new1.com, the site for my personal mentorship program. We built this program to help professionals be successful in their careers, in the technology aspects as well as the people and personal ones. (When was the last time you took a good stretch and cared about yourself a little bit?)

Just to make the setup clear: the prodbytes group on Substack is where we do group activities, one to many. And new1.com is where the one-to-one sessions and mentorship happen. But this could be any business you’d like. Think about how you could apply this to your own project, your own situation.

The point is that everything on new1.com runs on Squarespace alone. No AWS, no Google Cloud, no Azure. Sometimes a simple tool is enough.

The alternatives

Squarespace is not the only option, of course. A lot of people use Wix, which is also a website builder with tools for all sorts of sites, and of course everything is powered by AI these days. It’s not only content: they have e-commerce, scheduling, lead management, portfolios, blogs, online courses, whatever you want to build.

Another alternative today is Lovable, which is more focused on the AI side: custom development without writing code, using AI to specify what you want. Squarespace sits somewhere in between, a bit of a website builder and a bit of an AI tool.

What it actually costs

One honest caveat: this route is not always less expensive. No secrets here, so let me show you exactly what I’m paying. My Squarespace site is $17 a month, plus $15 a year for the domain. For that, I get all the settings for the website, the domain, email, and branding in one place, everything I need to keep the site running.

And if I do know how to code and want to, there are developer tools for integrations, for protecting the site, and for adding custom code. They are there when you need them, and invisible when you don’t.

Editing without fear

The main part is the website section, where the pages live. The pages in the main navigation are the ones that show up on the navigation bar; the homepage and a few hidden pages sit outside it.

Editing is genuinely simple. If I want to change my workshops page, I open the editor and the page is a series of sections. For each section there are templates for text, forms, contact information, presenting your team, and selling your products and services. Adding an element is just a matter of picking it and configuring it, and replacing an image is clicking the pen icon and browsing for a new one. That’s how easy it is to customize your website. There is no build step, no deploy, and no fear of breaking something you don’t understand.

Beyond the pages, there are marketing tools for announcements and for controlling how things look when you share them, and website styles where you set your color palette to match your brand (probably not my best skill, but the tools are there). If you do scheduling for sessions, charge for services, run online courses, take donations, or send invoices, everything can be done right there, without much complication or trouble.

There’s even a custom code injection option for little bits of code to change how things look or behave. To be honest, I don’t usually even do that. The built-in tools are more than enough.

Own your domain, own your content

For me, the really important part is tying the site to my own domain. When you have your own domain, your content is kind of your property. Your links, and everything you share, live under a name you control, not only on one platform like YouTube or LinkedIn.

Because, well, sometimes YouTube can change the rules on you. Sometimes LinkedIn can become a sea of useless content. And perhaps you want the content you build to serve your own growth, wherever the platforms go.

So that’s one way to do it. I can recommend Squarespace from real use, and the alternatives are great as well. Whether you code or not, you can build, sell, and share whatever you want online, keeping your brand and your identity.
